The idea behind Forgotten Heroes is simple: simulate a D&D-like world, with NPCs roaming around and plausible market dynamics.

The gameplay should be simple, mainly consisting of clicking buttons and choosing menu options, with text descriptions of what is happening, simple graph node visualizations, some static imagery, etc. However, the level of challenge should be significant: this is a magical but gritty world, where characters are no strangers to disappointment, failure, and even death.

The main gameplay loop is not revolving around dungeon delving or combat - there are plenty of other games for that - but rather the out-of-dungeon life of adventurers, trying to make a profit while engaging with curious encounters along the road. The vibe I'm trying to strike is something like "Spice & Wolf" or "Frieren", but with mechanics rooted in D&D 5e. The party size is variable; initially only 1-3 characters. Growing the party has the benefit of being able to handle more types of situations and deter enemies from stealing your goods, but consumables and camaraderie are harder to maintain with more people.

Almost everything in the game should be input-random: the odds, DCs, and outcomes are clearly laid out to the player, and it is up to them to decide which path to pursue.

The emphasis is on gritty travel, discovering locations, and strategy. I'm kind of imagining something like Battle Brothers or Mount & Blade, but where settlement/party size matters, and the world is a lot more magical and alive (populations and settlements actually change over time or through wars). The breadth and impact of out-of-combat activities is greatly increased; there are multiple resources to manage. Just amassing gold shouldn't be enough to survive. More types of resources and additional complexity is gradually introduced as the party levels up, and management of mundane resources increasingly automated or abstracted away as they find magical solutions or investments for dealing with basic needs.

As an example, the party might, at some point, become leaders of a small group of mercenaries, and manage things at the town/quest level instead of as mere individuals. Or they might buy a shop at one of the many towns in the world, but need to travel to help find ingredients as the shop is pushing their demand and price up. Details remain to be hashed out; the base party gameplay needs to be polished first.

Dungeons should be simplified in comparison. I'm a bit undecided on the details of this, but generally something like donjon's dungeon generator could be a good start; open a room and deal with what's within. Handling of traps and "alarm level" is equally as important as actual fighting. There should ideally be at least 2 or 3 options for how to handle each encounter, with fighting being one of them but other semi-random options being available and potentially more attractive while the alarm level is low.

Actual fights can be simplified: distance between units is tracked on a linear distance tracker (roughly 6-8 tiles long, with units stacking on tiles, units being initially placed depending on formation, max size depending on the room "shape", etc). Flanking is replaced by assisting. Units can generally move 1 point and attack, or do something resembling a full attack. Other abilities and actions need to be fit into this structure, but generally superfluous (non-class specific) actions can be similarly simplified or removed. Running away should be an option, but not without its risks. The goal is to make combat into a deep strategic decision, but simple and quick-to-play in practice. The options available should reflect the characters' combat and skill prowess. Generally combat itself should have no reward; clearing a dungeon floor or similar (regardless of methods) is the goal.

Critical success (nat 20) in non-combat actions should enable that character to gain an inspiration point, spendable similar to 5e.

I'm a fan of "skill challenges", "alarm level", "soft time limits", and other ways of measuring success/failure with partial and gradual levels, allowing the player to re-evaluate their situation and react accordingly. Again, the information regarding the options and outcomes should be presented with some visibility ahead of time. Similarly, the cost of failure should only be death in extreme situations: perhaps a temporary or permanent injury, loss of money or a magic item, or other hindrance suits lower-stakes adventures better.

More notes of my preferred gameplay are at solo-dm-notes.txt, but note that it is geared towards tabletop gameplay, with more complex combat and less trackable resources than Forgotten Heroes is intended for. The reason is that playing a video game allows tracking of arbitrary number of things and is uniquely suited for an overworld adventure focused simulator; doing it on tabletop would be tedious. Some ideas can be borrowed or reimagined from there and other solo D&D experiences: surprise me!
